# Algebraic closure and finite generated field extensions: complete proof

## 0. Setting and terminology

Let F be a field and let E/F be a field extension. An element α in E is **algebraic over F** if there is a nonzero polynomial p in F[X] such that p(α)=0. Its monic irreducible polynomial of least positive degree is its **minimal polynomial**, denoted m_(α,F).

For a finite list α₁,...,αᵣ, the notation F(α₁,...,αᵣ) means the smallest intermediate field containing F and all the αᵢ. It is field generation, not merely polynomial-ring generation.

The degree [L:F] of an extension is the dimension of L as an F-vector space.

## 1. Inversion preserves algebraicity

**Theorem 1.** If α is algebraic over F and α ≠ 0, then α⁻¹ is algebraic over F.

**Proof.** Let

\[
m_{\alpha,F}(X)=a_0+a_1X+\cdots+a_dX^d
\]

be the minimal polynomial of α over F. Since α ≠ 0, its constant coefficient is nonzero. Indeed, if a₀=0, then X divides the irreducible polynomial m_(α,F); hence m_(α,F) is a scalar multiple of X, forcing α=0, a contradiction.

Define the reversed polynomial

\[
q(Y)=a_0Y^d+a_1Y^{d-1}+\cdots+a_d.
\]

Its leading coefficient is a₀ ≠ 0, so q is not the zero polynomial. Since m_(α,F)(α)=0 and α ≠ 0,

\[
q(\alpha^{-1})
 =\alpha^{-d}m_{\alpha,F}(\alpha)
 =0.
\]

Equivalently, q(Y)=Y^d m_(α,F)(Y⁻¹), evaluated at Y=α⁻¹. Thus α⁻¹ is a root of a nonzero polynomial over F and is algebraic. ∎

## 2. Every integer power preserves algebraicity

**Theorem 2.** If α is algebraic over F, then αⁿ is algebraic over F for every integer n for which the power is defined in the ordinary field convention. In particular, negative n require α ≠ 0.

**Proof.** Set K=F(α). The simple-extension theorem gives

\[
[K:F]=\deg m_{\alpha,F}<\infty.
\]

Every nonnegative power αⁿ belongs to K. If α ≠ 0, Theorem 1 gives α⁻¹ in K, so every negative power also belongs to K. By Theorem 4 below, every element of the finite-dimensional extension K/F is algebraic over F. Hence αⁿ is algebraic for all permitted integers n. ∎

A second route for n≥0 is simply that αⁿ is an element of F(α); the finite-dimensionality argument treats positive and negative powers uniformly once inversion has been established.

## 3. Finitely many algebraic generators give a finite-dimensional extension

**Theorem 3.** If α₁,...,αᵣ are algebraic over F, then

\[
K=F(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r)
\]

is finite-dimensional over F. More precisely, if dᵢ is the degree of the minimal polynomial of αᵢ over F, then

\[
[K:F]\leq \prod_{i=1}^{r}d_i.
\]

**Proof.** Put K₀=F and Kᵢ=Kᵢ₋₁(αᵢ). Let mᵢ(X) be the minimal polynomial of αᵢ over F, of degree dᵢ.

The coefficient field Kᵢ₋₁ contains F. Thus the same polynomial mᵢ, regarded as a polynomial over Kᵢ₋₁, is still nonzero and still has αᵢ as a root. Let μᵢ be the minimal polynomial of αᵢ over Kᵢ₋₁. It divides mᵢ in Kᵢ₋₁[X], so

\[
\deg\mu_i\leq d_i.
\]

By the simple-extension degree theorem,

\[
[K_i:K_{i-1}]=\deg\mu_i\leq d_i.
\]

Repeated use of the tower law now gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
[K:F]
 &= [K_r:K_{r-1}]\cdots[K_1:K_0]\\
 &\leq d_r\cdots d_1
 =\prod_{i=1}^{r}d_i
 <\infty.
\end{aligned}
\]

This proves finite-dimensionality and the stated degree bound. ∎

### The finite monomial certificate

The same proof supplies an explicit finite spanning family:

\[
\mathcal M=
\left\{
\alpha_1^{e_1}\cdots\alpha_r^{e_r}
\mid 0\leq e_i<d_i
\right\}.
\]

At the i-th step, the powers 1,αᵢ,...,αᵢ^(dᵢ−1) span Kᵢ over Kᵢ₋₁ because the minimal polynomial over Kᵢ₋₁ has degree at most dᵢ. Induction through the tower shows that the products in 𝓜 span K over F. There are at most ∏dᵢ such products.

They are not asserted to be linearly independent; the inequality is therefore the correct general statement. This is a finite certificate for the dimension bound.

## 4. Every element of a finite-dimensional extension is algebraic

**Theorem 4.** If L/F is a finite-dimensional field extension, then every x in L is algebraic over F.

**Proof.** Let N=[L:F]. The N+1 elements

\[
1,x,x^2,\ldots,x^N
\]

belong to the N-dimensional F-vector space L. They are linearly dependent. Therefore there exist c₀,...,c_N in F, not all zero, such that

\[
c_0+c_1x+\cdots+c_Nx^N=0.
\]

The polynomial p(X)=c₀+c₁X+⋯+c_NXᴺ is nonzero because its coefficients are not all zero, and p(x)=0. Thus x is algebraic over F. ∎

## 5. Finite generation plus algebraicity is equivalent to finite degree

**Theorem 5.** For a field extension L/F, the following are equivalent:

1. L/F is finite-dimensional.
2. L/F is algebraic and L is generated by finitely many elements as a field over F.

**Proof.**

(1 ⇒ 2) By Theorem 4, every element of L is algebraic over F. Choose a finite F-basis b₁,...,b_N of L. The field F(b₁,...,b_N) contains every basis element and F, hence every F-linear combination of the basis. Since every element of L is such a combination,

\[
L=F(b_1,\ldots,b_N).
\]

So L/F is finitely generated as a field and algebraic.

(2 ⇒ 1) Choose algebraic generators α₁,...,αᵣ of L/F. Then L=F(α₁,...,αᵣ), and Theorem 3 gives

\[
[L:F]\leq\prod_i\deg m_{\alpha_i,F}<\infty.
\]

Thus L/F is finite-dimensional. ∎

**Terminology warning.** “Finitely generated as a field” and “finitely generated as an F-algebra” are different notions. The criterion above uses field generation plus algebraicity. In Mathlib, the field-generation side is represented by Algebra.EssFiniteType; the theorem IntermediateField.fg_top_iff identifies it with finite generation of the top intermediate field. Mathlib then packages the reverse implication in Algebra.finite_of_essFiniteType_of_isAlgebraic.

## 6. Dependence between the requested closure properties

The three requested statements now follow from the same finite-dimensionality mechanism:

- For nonzero algebraic α, Theorem 1 gives α⁻¹ algebraic.
- For any permitted integer n, αⁿ lies in the finite extension F(α), hence is algebraic; this is Theorem 2.
- For finitely many algebraic αᵢ, Theorem 3 gives finite-dimensionality of their compositum.
- Theorem 4 supplies the bridge from finite-dimensionality back to algebraicity, and Theorem 5 states the finite-generation criterion exactly.

No separability assumption is used. No primitive-element theorem is used: the tower K₀⊆K₁⊆⋯⊆Kᵣ works even for inseparable extensions.

## 7. Boundary example: algebraic does not mean finite

The algebraic extension \(\overline{\mathbb Q}/\mathbb Q\) is not finite-dimensional. For every prime p, the polynomial Xᵖ−2 is Eisenstein at 2 and therefore irreducible over Q. Thus \(\mathbb Q(2^{1/p})/\mathbb Q\) has degree p. Since primes p are unbounded, the degrees of subextensions of \(\overline{\mathbb Q}/\mathbb Q\) are unbounded. Consequently \([\overline{\mathbb Q}:\mathbb Q]=\infty\).

This shows why finite generation is essential in Theorem 5.

## 8. Exact computational sanity checks (not proofs)

Wolfram's exact minimal-polynomial calculations give:

\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
x & \text{minimal polynomial over }\mathbb Q\\
\hline
1/\sqrt 2 & 2X^2-1\\
(\sqrt 2)^{-3} & 8X^2-1\\
(\sqrt 2)^5 & X^2-32\\
\sqrt 2+\sqrt 3 & X^4-10X^2+1
\end{array}
\]

These illustrate the inverse, integer-power, and compositum constructions. They are sanity checks on examples only; the proofs above do not depend on computation.
